01

What merchants don't know is costing them millions

Not a design brief. A real problem in a room I walked into with no map.

Every month, somewhere in the back office of a large retailer or payment processor, a finance analyst opens a 200-page PDF from their acquirer. The document is dense with fee categories, tier classifications, and interchange qualifiers. The analyst knows the total. They don't know why.

Why did this month cost more than last? Which transaction types are being downgraded? Is there a data quality issue causing the spike? The invoice offers no answers. The acquirer, when asked, responds with reference to their terms. The merchant absorbs the cost — again.

This is not a niche problem. Interchange fees — which vary across 300+ rate categories based on transaction data quality, settlement timing, card type, and merchant category — represent the largest single operating cost after labour and rent for many businesses.[1] A single downgrade moves a credit card from a 1.8% target rate to Visa's EIRF fallback at 2.30% + $0.10, or worse, to the Standard tier at 2.70% + $0.10.[2] At enterprise scale, that's not a rounding error. It's a P&L line that nobody can explain.

"A merchant processing $50M monthly GMV could be leaking $200K–$300K to preventable downgrades — and have no tool to see it, trace it, or fix it."

The problem we set out to solve

HyperSense was our answer. And this is the story of how I designed it — from the first confused whiteboard session, through three weeks of domain immersion, to the product that landed four enterprise pilots including a demo to Apple.

02

Understanding the terrain

Before any design could happen, I had to understand why this domain was so hard — and why nobody had solved it yet.

IC++ (Interchange plus plus) is how large merchants and PSPs are billed for card transactions. Every payment passes through six distinct phases — checkout, 3D Secure, authorisation, capture, clearing, and settlement — with fees extracted at different points by different entities. Most merchants see only the final net payout. They never see this:

What makes this genuinely alarming is that the opacity isn't accidental. The current system "penalises complexity without explaining it. A merchant may upgrade their checkout experience only to find that their effective processing costs increase because transactions are being classified differently. The rules are not intuitive, and they are rarely transparent."[3]

03

Who was suffering — and how

Three very different users, one shared problem: they're all flying blind in a domain that should be fully observable.

Payment Ops Manager
Knows something is wrong. Spends hours cross-referencing invoice PDFs with internal transaction logs in Excel. Has gut feel but no proof. Cannot get budget to fix a problem they can't quantify.
Finance Director
Sees the number on the P&L. Has no lever to pull. Asks "why did processing costs go up 8%?" and receives a shrug. Cannot hold any party accountable because there is no evidence trail.
PSP Account Manager
Manages 50+ merchant accounts. Wants to add value in client reviews but has no tools. Gives generic advice about "optimizing processing." Loses clients to competitors who can show more.

What united all three was not a feature request — it was a question. Why is this costing what it costs, and where is the money going? No existing tool answered that question clearly. HyperSense had to.

These weren't just personas to inform a UX flow. Each user type had a fundamentally different relationship with the data: the ops manager needed transaction-level granularity; the finance director needed narrative summaries; the PSP account manager needed portfolio-wide comparisons. One product had to work for all three without becoming three different products.

04

Why we built this — not for revenue

The strategic question behind any 0→1 product: why this, why now, why us?

HyperSense wasn't initiated as a revenue play. The initial reasoning was more nuanced: if Hyperswitch, Juspay's open-source payment orchestration platform, was going to win enterprise merchants, those merchants needed to trust that switching infrastructure providers wouldn't create new visibility problems.

Cost transparency was a trust signal. A merchant weighing a switch from their existing PSP would ask: "Will I understand my costs better or worse with you?" HyperSense was the answer to that question — proof that the infrastructure layer came with intelligence attached.

"We weren't building a dashboard. We were building the case for why better infrastructure is worth the migration pain."

Strategic framing — early BD conversation

This framing shaped every design decision downstream. HyperSense had to feel like an enterprise product from the first interaction — not an analytics tool bolted onto something else, but a primary interface for a genuinely new category: payment cost intelligence.

05

What made this genuinely hard to design

Not technically complex. Hard in the way that makes you rethink your process from the start.

1
No existing mental model — for anyone

Nobody had built a cost observability product for interchange at this depth before. There were no competitor screens to audit, no established interaction patterns, no UX precedent. The closest analogs were cloud cost dashboards (AWS Cost Explorer, Datadog) and financial analytics tools — but neither mapped cleanly onto the problem. I had to define what "good" looked like before I could design toward it.

2
The domain vocabulary was alienating — structurally so

Interchange fee structures aren't just complex — they're deliberately opaque. Card network fee schedules run hundreds of pages and are updated twice yearly, often without clear merchant notification. Terms like "downgrade," "EIRF," "qualification program," and "EMV fallback" have no intuitive meaning, even for many people working inside payment companies. Designing for this domain meant being an educator as much as a designer. Every screen had to teach without patronising — and without reproducing the exact opacity it was trying to solve.

3
Three user types — three completely different mental models

A payment ops manager needed transaction-level root cause analysis. A finance director needed a P&L narrative with clear cost attribution. A PSP account manager needed portfolio-level insights across multiple merchants. These weren't different levels of the same view — they were fundamentally different relationships with the data. One product architecture had to serve all three without fracturing into three different tools.

4
AI was central — not a feature, but the product's core intelligence

The RCA engine, anomaly detection, AI chatbot, and ML-based fee classification were not add-ons. They were how the product worked. Designing interfaces for AI outputs meant designing for confidence levels, explanations, and the edge case where the system doesn't know — without undermining trust or creating over-reliance. This required a new design vocabulary I had to invent as I went.

06

How I approached it

When the domain is foreign, the worst thing you can do is start with wireframes.

I started where I always start when the domain is genuinely unfamiliar: deep listening, not sketching. Three weeks of discovery sessions with the BD team, payment ops managers, and two enterprise merchant finance teams gave me a picture of what people actually did today.

They scraped data manually from invoice PDFs, built elaborate Excel models to track fee trends month-over-month, and relied entirely on their acquirer's account manager for interpretation — a person who themselves had limited insight into why individual transactions downgraded. The "best practice" was a workaround built on top of a workaround.

Phase 01 — Discovery
Learn the domain before the product

Deep dives with BD, payment ops managers, and merchant finance teams. Reading actual Visa/Mastercard fee schedules. Building a glossary as a shared language between design, PM, and engineering.

Phase 02 — Synthesis
Map the question hierarchy

Instead of a sitemap, I built a hierarchy of questions the product needed to answer — from most strategic to most granular. These five questions became the spine of the product's information architecture.

Phase 03 — Architecture
Define structure before screens

Information architecture that accounted for three user types, multiple entry points, and a data model with 50+ downgrade reasons. The IA work took longer than the initial wireframes — intentionally.

Phase 04 — Craft
Design for clarity, not comprehensiveness

The temptation in analytics products is to show everything. I worked against that instinct — every screen went through a "what's the one decision this helps you make?" filter before it shipped.

The Spine — 5 questions that structured the product

① How much am I paying in total?  →  ② Is it going up or down, and why?  →  ③ Which transaction types cost the most?  →  ④ What specifically caused each downgrade?  →  ⑤ What would it cost to fix them?

These weren't product features. They were the questions merchants actually needed to answer, in the order they naturally asked them. The product flow followed this sequence.

07

The decisions that shaped the design

Not a list of features. The specific design choices where I made a call — and why that call mattered.

Design Decision 01
The RCA output needed to be a story, not a table

When the Root Cause Analysis engine identifies why a transaction downgraded, it maps it to one of 50+ possible reasons. The first instinct — from engineering and PM — was to show this as a data table. A transaction ID, a downgrade reason code, a fee delta.

I pushed back. The user receiving this output is a payment ops manager who has been staring at unexplained numbers for months. They don't need a code. They need to understand what went wrong in human terms, what it cost, and what they can do about it. The final RCA design layered the output: a plain-English summary generated by GPT-4 at the top, followed by the technical breakdown for those who needed it. This pattern — explanation first, evidence second — became a principle across the entire product.

Design Decision 02
Anomaly detection needed a confidence signal, not just an alert

Anomaly detection in a payments context is high-stakes. A false positive — an alert that sends a team into investigation mode for a spike that turns out to be seasonal — erodes trust fast. After the third false alarm, users start ignoring alerts entirely.

I designed the anomaly alert system with an explicit confidence indicator alongside every alert. Not a percentage (which reads as false precision) but a contextual signal: "this pattern is unusual against a 90-day baseline" versus "this may be seasonal — we've seen similar patterns in prior periods." The goal was calibrated trust: users should rely on alerts that deserve reliance, and be appropriately skeptical of those that don't.

Design Decision 03
The AI chatbot had to be an analyst, not an oracle

The product included a RAG-based chatbot that could answer questions in plain English about the merchant's own transaction data. The design risk was obvious: if it felt like "ask anything," users would ask everything, get inconsistent answers, and lose confidence in the product entirely.

I scoped the chatbot explicitly as a scoped analyst, not a general assistant. The entry point surfaced it with suggested questions derived from the merchant's actual data — "Your settlement timing is causing downgrades on 23% of Visa transactions. Want to understand why?" This pattern (prompt from the data, not from the user) dramatically increased meaningful engagement and reduced the noise that comes from open-ended prompting.

Design Decision 04
The dashboard had to tell a cost narrative, not show all the numbers

Analytics products have a default failure mode: showing everything, organized by data type rather than by question. You get GMV here, fee rate there, transaction count somewhere else — and the user has to do the synthesis work themselves.

I designed the HyperSense dashboard around a single primary question: Is my cost trend healthy? Everything above the fold answered that question. Detailed breakdowns lived one level down, accessible but not dominant. The layout was intentionally sequential — you read it like a brief, not scan it like a spreadsheet.

10

What I'd do differently

Honesty is more useful than polish when you're trying to get better.

I'd invest more in onboarding earlier Domain education was implicit in the product — embedded in tooltips and contextual explanations. But the gap between a merchant's mental model on day one and what the product assumed they knew was larger than I anticipated. A more structured first-run experience, built around the "5 questions" framework from the start, would have reduced time-to-value significantly.
I should have pressure-tested the RCA output format earlier The decision to lead with plain-English explanations over technical tables was made on reasoning, not user testing. It turned out to be the right call, but I validated it late — after the design was largely committed. Moving user testing of the RCA output to week three rather than week eight would have given me more room to iterate on the format.
The three-user-type problem deserves its own design system I managed the divergent needs of ops managers, finance directors, and PSP account managers through content hierarchy and progressive disclosure — which worked, but created maintenance complexity as the product grew. A more explicit role-based navigation model, explored as a design option from the start, might have been more scalable.

The deepest thing this project taught me: designing for a domain with no prior mental model requires you to be a researcher and an educator long before you're a designer. The biggest risk isn't building the wrong UI — it's building the right UI for a problem the user hasn't yet been helped to see clearly.

11

What's next

The product is live. The design work isn't done.

Several directions are in active exploration. Scenario analysis — "what would you save if you fixed the top three downgrade reasons?" — is the most strategically important. It shifts HyperSense from a diagnostic tool to a planning tool: not just understanding cost, but modelling the upside of fixing it. The design challenge here is substantial: how do you show a counterfactual clearly enough to be credible, without overselling a projection?

The fee rate manager — which tracks Visa and Mastercard fee schedule changes quarterly and surfaces their impact on each merchant — is another area where the design problem is mostly a communication problem. Fee schedule updates are dense and technical. The design task is translating them into plain-language impact assessments, specific to each merchant's transaction profile.

And there's a longer-horizon question I find genuinely interesting: what does a mobile-first version of this product look like for a finance director who wants a cost narrative in their morning brief — not an analytics dashboard, but a five-sentence summary with one decision to make? That's a different kind of product design challenge, and one worth exploring.
